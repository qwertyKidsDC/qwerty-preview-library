I still remember making my first link.

It was 1997. I was at university, the web was still a very strange place, and my link went to NASA.

Of course it went to NASA.

I remember the click. Mostly, I remember the buzz of realising that something had happened on the screen because I made it happen.

I still get that feeling. Not quite with the force of the first one. That would be exhausting. But almost every time I make something work, there is still a tiny ohhh, look at that.

I nearly avoided the whole thing

I started a media degree in 1996 as a mature-age student who hadn’t finished high school. I thought I might become a journalist. Maybe a director.

One lecturer told us about an extraordinary new resource called the internet, or the web, or possibly both. I was already slightly terrified by university and had only just worked out the library, so I deliberately left the new-fangled internet alone for a year.

A strong start to a career in technology.

Then I took a desktop publishing unit. A lot of people seemed confused by it.

I wasn’t.

My thinking was roughly: well, this appears to be difficult for everyone else. Perhaps I should do more of it.

I ended up graduating equal first in my year. My eventual ambition was to work for the ABC, once I had enough experience.

My first job was at the ABC.

The first lab

I became a web developer at ABC Science Online and worked at The Lab from 2000 to 2007.

Qwerty Labs isn’t named after it. That is just a rather excellent coincidence.

The Lab was a place to experiment, at a time before social media managers, UX teams, podcast producers and quite a few other job titles we now take for granted.

Often the question wasn’t simply how do we build this?

It was: What even is this? And how could people use it?

I worked on online communities, interactive science, emerging audio and video, information architecture, user experience and whatever else turned up next.

I moderated the Self-Service Science Forum, where the earliest documented use of the word selfie appeared. There is a very good chance I pushed that post live, although nobody realised we were supposed to preserve the moment for future dictionaries.

I also worked on projects for Dr Karl Kruszelnicki and the Surfing Scientist, produced early podcast work for Dr Karl, and later designed the user experience for Radio National’s enormous podcast library.

The technology kept changing. The interesting question didn’t:

What can we do with this that is useful, interesting or just a bit wonderful?

Sometimes the right thing to build is nothing

GetUp! once commissioned me to design an events system to help it reach more young people.

I did the information architecture. They loved it.

Then I showed them Facebook and enthusiastically suggested Twitter.

They paid me for the IA and didn’t build the product.

This remains one of my favourite projects.

Technology is a tool, not an obligation. Sometimes the right answer is a beautifully designed new system.

Sometimes it is: you already have what you need.

Then things got bigger

At Microsoft Australia I worked across its developer, IT professional and education communities.

For TechEd Australia, three major marketing agencies pitched for a delegate-engagement project. I put an idea forward too.

That was the one that went ahead.

We built a Windows 10-style event website and a giant community screen that made activity across the conference visible and participatory. The website started turning up as the lead image in stories about TechEd, despite there being thousands of perfectly photographable humans at the event.

Gizmodo interviewed me about the community screen, and versions of the idea later appeared at other TechEd events around the world.

For the 2012 Imagine Cup world finals in Sydney, I designed the UX and leaderboard for the event app, then spent the event encouraging people to actually use it.

I have never been terribly interested in technology that works beautifully in a meeting room and nowhere else.

Put it in front of people. See what they do. Then you know something.

And eventually, Qwerty

When I started QwertyKids in 2015, I had a fairly ridiculous ambition.

I wanted to create something like The Lab for children.

A place where they could encounter interesting technology, make things with it, follow ideas, break things occasionally, ask peculiar questions and discover what they were capable of doing.

It has taken ten years to properly understand how big that idea was.

Because it turns out it was never only about children. And it was never really only about coding.

Technology wanders through art, music, games, electronics, storytelling, design, AI, robotics, physical computing, science and things that don’t fit neatly under any heading at all.

Good.

That is why QwertyKids has become Qwerty Labs.

The lab is a place to learn, certainly. But also to experiment, build, investigate, make strange connections and occasionally follow an idea simply because somebody says:

“Hang on. What if we tried this?”

That first hyperlink to NASA was nearly thirty years ago.

I’m still chasing the click.
